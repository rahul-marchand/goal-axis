Does the value axis need a base that learned the task?
======================================================

The offline campaign finds a value axis in a route model trained on 900k
demonstrations (`bcnv11.s1`, sweep o0): arms on opposite sides of the base
oppose, an axis fitted without an arm reads that arm's offset back, and writing
offset * axis into the base moves its exchange rate. This asks whether any of
that requires the base to have learned the task.

Design. A rung is a base trained on N demonstrations and nothing else, with all
25 o0 arms fine-tuned on *the same N levels* at shifted values, so an arm's diff
is what changing a value does with the data held fixed. `bcnv11.s1.n<N>` is the
control: bcnv11's own parameters with arms restricted to those same N levels,
which separates "the base is a memoriser" from "the arms had little data".
Arms keep the campaign's budget and rate (1000 steps, batch 256, constant 1e-4
after 50 warmup). Behaviour is read on test.rho100 minus the arm pool's train
split, the only set held out from both pools; see RUNS.toml.

    scripts/overfit_axis_chain.sh, TAGS="1k 10k"; scripts/overfit_axis_report.py

Summary.

1. The axis is there in a memoriser, and the three geometric columns cannot
   tell any of these five conditions apart. They track how quiet the fine-tune
   was -- smaller arm sets are less noisy, so reliability *rises* to 0.987 for
   1k arms whether or not the base learned anything -- not what the base knows.

2. What separates them is behavioural, and specifically the axis written
   *without* its drift. It moves a threshold the base computes on levels it has
   not memorised, and does not move one the base has memorised. That holds in
   both directions: the trained base's write works on the arms' 1k/10k levels
   (which it never trained on), and the memorisers' writes fail there.

3. It is a gradient, not a cliff. Held-out, written tracks arms at 0.3/0.3 (1k),
   3.6/5.8 (10k) and 25.4/21.9 (900k) steps per unit value. The write can only
   move what the base already computes on unseen mazes.

4. Why the memorised levels resist: they are held ~6.5 logits harder than
   anything else the same model does (max softmax 1.0000 against 0.973), while
   the trained base is equally soft everywhere. The drift -- large, common to
   every arm, behaviourally inert on its own -- is what makes stored decisions
   movable again, after which the graded part expresses itself (memorised:
   written +0.6, +drift +15.6 at 1k; +1.7 and +16.6 at 10k). The 1k write has a
   deadband for |offset| <= 0.2 and responds only past 0.3, which is what a
   threshold-crossing account predicts.

Supporting diagnostics
----------------------

What survives memorisation is the maze half of the task, not the value half.
bcov11.n1k.s1 decoded on 25,000 held-out levels (3,937 completed episodes):

    reached the BFS-nearer objective        0.913
    reached the richer objective            0.500   (chance)
    of 227 episodes where BFS-nearest and Manhattan-nearest differ:
      went to the BFS-nearer                0.850
      went to the Manhattan-nearer          0.150

So its held-out policy is shortest-path-to-nearest, walls included, with an
exchange rate of zero. The held-out null is therefore a real negative -- there
is a threshold there to move -- and not a definitional one. Caveat: it finishes
only 15.7% of held-out routes and the rest are unscored.

Saturation, teacher-forced, mean over action positions (1024 levels per set):

    base              own levels                 held-out
    bcov11.n1k.s1     gap 19.20  max prob 1.0000  gap 12.24  max prob 0.9726
    bcov11.n10k.s1    gap 19.36  max prob 1.0000  gap 14.60  max prob 0.9733
    bcnv11.s1         gap 12.92  max prob 0.9894  gap 12.87  max prob 0.9895

Evidence for the account in 4, not proof of it: the margin is over all action
positions rather than over the choice of objective.
